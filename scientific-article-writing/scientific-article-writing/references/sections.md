# Section guide

For each section: the reader's question, the moves in default order, what the section must not do, the failure modes reviewers catch, and the audit before returning it. Moves are defaults; adapt to the venue's model, never to the exemplars' wording.

---

## Title

**Reader question:** What does this paper promise?

**Moves**

- Fewest words that fully describe the content and still attract the right reader.
- Name the finding or the question, the system or population when it bounds scope, and the design when it changes inference (for example an intervention versus an observational study).
- Three accepted forms: a declarative finding, a noun phrase naming the phenomenon and its context, or a gerund phrase naming what the paper does. Prefer the declarative form when the design supports the claim.

**Do not**

- use causal verbs for observational designs;
- use `novel`, `first`, or `comprehensive` without verification;
- use abbreviations the field does not universally share;
- promise mechanism, generality, or clinical utility the paper does not deliver;
- write the title before the body exists.

**Diagnostic:** send the title alone to several uninvolved readers and ask what they expect inside. Divergent answers mean ambiguity; convergent but wrong answers mean a misleading title.

**Audit:** map every title element to objective, design, population, variables, evidence, and conclusion. Remove or qualify anything unsupported.

## Abstract

**Reader question:** Should I read this, and what will I learn?

**Moves (default six, 150 to 250 words unless the venue says otherwise)**

1. Context in one or two sentences: what is established and why it matters.
2. The gap: what remains unclear, contested, or untested. One sentence.
3. What was done here: design, sample or system, manipulation, and measure, compact but identifying.
4. Findings: specific and directional. Name which condition, region, group, or variable did what. Report the primary result with magnitude and uncertainty when the materials supply them; report the important null result when it changes interpretation.
5. Calibrated conclusion: what the findings together support, at the design's strength.
6. Implication: why the field should care, in one sentence.

**Formats:** informative (moves without headings), structured (venue headings such as Background, Methods, Results, Conclusions), or a short summary (venue-specific, often around 150 words with fewer numbers and more conceptual framing). Match the venue.

**Do not**

- open with background that consumes space needed for findings;
- write vague result sentences when numbers were supplied;
- include citations unless the venue requires them;
- make the abstract stronger than Results and Discussion;
- omit the sample or setting while generalizing.

**Diagnostic:** ask uninvolved readers whether the abstract is jargon-free, whether the research interests them, and whether they would pay to read the full article.

**Audit:** cross-check every fact against the manuscript. Verify objective, design, result direction, sample, limitation, and word limit.

## Highlights and summary blurb

Some venues require three to five highlight bullets and a short third-person blurb.

- Each highlight is one finding in one line, within the venue's character limit, phrased as a result rather than a topic.
- The blurb names the authors in third person, states the approach in a clause, and states the main conclusion and its significance in one or two sentences.
- Neither may contain claims absent from the abstract.

## Introduction

**Reader question:** Why was this study needed, and what exactly did it test?

**Length:** typically four to six paragraphs, at most about two pages.

**Moves**

1. **Territory.** Open on the phenomenon or problem at the level the venue's readers share. A concrete, engaging first sentence is acceptable in high-impact venues; a universal claim too broad for the evidence is not.
2. **What is known.** Synthesize prior work by idea, not paper by paper. Cite only the most relevant work.
3. **What is unresolved.** State competing accounts or the untested implication. High-impact papers frequently frame this as two explicit alternatives with different predictions.
4. **The gap and why it matters.** Say what remains unknown and what goes wrong if it stays unknown. "No one has done this" is not a justification.
5. **Present study.** State the aim, the approach in one or two sentences, and explicit predictions or hypotheses. Where several hypotheses are tested, number them; Results then follows the same order.

Scope moves general -> particular -> general: from the field's problem down to this study, then back out to what the answer changes.

**Hypotheses, where used:** non-obvious, singular, directional, derived from the framework, and testable with the chosen design and sample. Avoid compound hypotheses, null hypotheses as predictions, sub-hypothesis inflation, and unspecified direction.

**Do not**

- review all literature regardless of relevance;
- report results or interpret them;
- introduce Methods detail needed only for replication;
- claim `no studies have examined` without verification;
- turn an exploratory question into a confirmatory prediction;
- add hypotheses because the section looks incomplete.

**Diagnostic:** ask uninvolved readers to name the research question, the framework, and the gap. Divergent answers mean the writing is unclear; correct answers plus indifference mean the gap needs a stronger case or a different venue.

**Audit:** the gap follows from the supplied literature; the aim matches the design; citations stay attached to their propositions; the final paragraph promises only what Results delivers.

## Methods

**Reader question:** What exactly was done, in what order, to whom or what, and how was it analyzed?

Two purposes: demonstrate rigor and enable replication. Methods fail if a careful reader cannot reproduce the study.

**Moves, using only supplied details**

1. **Participants or system.** Recruitment source, number recruited, screening and exclusion criteria, number excluded with reasons, final analyzed number, demographics, ethics approval, consent, and safety guideline compliance where relevant.
2. **Design.** Conditions, factors, within- or between-subject structure, counterbalancing, blinding, preregistration if any.
3. **Materials and stimuli.** What was presented or applied, how it was created, and how it was delivered.
4. **Procedure.** Chronological, session by session, with timings.
5. **Acquisition.** Equipment with manufacturer, model, and settings; recording parameters; targeting or localization procedure; sensory or environmental controls.
6. **Preprocessing.** Steps in order, with software names and versions, thresholds, rejection criteria, and how many trials or channels were removed.
7. **Analysis.** Outcome measures and how they were computed; models and their terms; statistical tests with assumption checks, multiple-comparison correction, and thresholds; distinction between prespecified and exploratory analyses; sample-size or power rationale when supplied.
8. **Availability.** Data and code locations.

Brief rationale sentences inside Methods are conventional and useful ("we chose X to match Y"), but only when the author supplied the rationale. Reference a prior paper for a pipeline only when the author did.

**Do not**

- fill missing details with field defaults;
- convert planned analyses into completed ones;
- rename variables or groups inconsistently with Results;
- add standard-sounding ethics or safety language the author did not document;
- justify a method with an invented rationale.

**Audit:** trace every procedural sentence to a supplied source. Verify counts, labels, versions, parameters, time points, thresholds, exclusions, tests, and chronology.

## Results

**Reader question:** What was found?

**Organization**

- One subsection per figure-claim pair. Headers state the claim ("Region X carried context information at encoding"), or, if the venue prefers topical headers, name the analysis precisely. Numbered experiments get their own subsections.
- Order follows the hypotheses or the logical chain; primary results before controls, controls before exploratory analyses.
- Open the section, if useful, with one short orienting paragraph: the design in two sentences, behavioral sanity checks, and where details live.

**Paragraph anatomy (four beats)**

1. **Aim.** What question this analysis answers, tied to the hypothesis where relevant.
2. **Approach.** One or two sentences on how, referencing Methods for detail.
3. **Result.** The finding with its statistic, uncertainty, and effect size inline, and the figure or table callout. Include null results with their statistics.
4. **Takeaway.** One sentence stating what the result shows, at the strength the design permits. In many empirical venues this brief interpretive sentence is conventional; a paragraph of interpretation is not.

**Also required**

- control analyses that rule out the obvious alternative, reported with the same rigor;
- replication in an independent sample or experiment, when available, reported as replication;
- exploratory analyses labeled as exploratory;
- speed-accuracy, artifact, or confound checks when the design invites the question.

**Do not**

- add mechanism, literature comparison, implications, or recommendations;
- infer that one variable matters more than another from one significant and one null test without a direct comparison;
- call a non-significant result a trend without authorization;
- duplicate every table value in prose; give the evidence hierarchy;
- reverse comparator or direction; change decimals, denominators, or intervals.

**Diagnostic:** a reader given only the subsection headers should be able to reconstruct the argument.

**Audit:** match every result to outcome, comparison, group, time point, estimate, uncertainty, significance, and source. Confirm null results are visible and no new explanation appears.

## Discussion

**Reader question:** What do the findings mean, what do they not mean, and why does the contribution matter?

**Moves**

1. **Findings summary.** Open with a paragraph that restates what was done and the main findings, often enumerated, using no stronger language than Results.
2. **Relation to prior work.** For each main finding: replicated, extended, or contradicted what, and with which specific difference in design. Use the author's supplied literature only.
3. **Interpretation.** What the pattern supports, at ladder strength. Distinguish the author's interpretation from prior views.
4. **Alternatives.** Name plausible alternative explanations the author identified and what evidence argues against them; say plainly what cannot be ruled out.
5. **Limitations.** Each limitation: what it is, why it matters for which claim, what was done to mitigate it, and what future work would resolve it. Some venues prefer a separate Limitations section; either way, limitations must change the reach of a claim, not sit as a token final sentence.
6. **Implications.** For theory, method, or practice, at the strength the evidence supports. Distinguish implication from recommendation.
7. **Bounded conclusion.** One paragraph: the answer to the question, the contribution, and its scope.

Thematic subheadings are acceptable in long Discussions and help distracted readers.

**Do not**

- introduce new results, terms, or ideas;
- restate Results without interpretation;
- treat association as causation;
- generalize beyond the sample, setting, task, or site;
- use non-quantitative intensifiers (`dramatic`, `massive`, `considerably`, `very`);
- invent mechanisms, confounders, or future study designs;
- add a limitation because it is conventional.

**Diagnostic:** list each question the Introduction raised; find the paragraph that answers it. Any unanswered question means the loop is open.

**Audit:** trace each interpretation to a reported result or explicit author judgment. Check comparison direction, citation attachment, claim strength, limitation-conclusion alignment, and null-result visibility.

## Conclusion

**Reader question:** What can the evidence support?

**Moves**

1. Answer the research question at the evidence-supported level.
2. State the contribution and its scope condition.
3. State an implication or next step only when supported.

**Do not**

- summarize the paper (the abstract does that);
- introduce new content, citations, or populations;
- undermine the work with reflexive `might`, `probably`, `maybe` where the evidence is firm;
- inflate with `transformative`, `paradigm`, `breakthrough`.

**Audit:** compare with objective, design, primary and null results, limitations, and abstract. No claim exceeds the Discussion.

## Figure legends

**Moves**

1. Title sentence: a declarative statement of what the figure shows, or a precise noun phrase.
2. Panel-by-panel description of what is plotted, with condition labels matching the text.
3. Statistical notation: what error bars or shading represent, n, test, correction, and significance markers.
4. Cross-references to supplementary figures or Methods where relevant.

A legend must let a reader understand the figure without the text. It must not add claims the Results do not make. Use `assets/figure-legend-template.md`.

## Back matter

Include what the venue requires: data and code availability with persistent locations; author contributions (often CRediT roles); funding; competing interests; acknowledgements; ethics statement if not in Methods; supplementary material pointer. Never invent an availability statement; if data are not shared, say what is available on request from whom.
