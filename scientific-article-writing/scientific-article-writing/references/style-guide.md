# Style guide: the voice of high-impact empirical articles

Sentence-level habits observed across the four exemplar articles (Neuron 2017, Current Biology 2020, Nature Communications 2023, Imaging Neuroscience 2024; locators in `exemplar-model.md`). Each entry names the move, what it does for the reader, when to use it, and a synthetic illustration. Illustrations are invented; no sentence below comes from a source. Use the moves as functions, never as a phrase bank to paste.

The style is confident, first-person, question-driven, and relentlessly calibrated. Every paragraph makes a reader-facing promise (what question is being answered), delivers evidence, and closes with what the evidence licenses.

## Voice

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| First-person plural for actions and interpretations | `We tested`, `We found`, `We reasoned`, `Our findings suggest`. Not `the authors`, not `this paper argues`. |
| Study self-reference alternates | `the present study`, `the current study`, `the present findings`, `our results`, `here`. The locative `Here, we ...` marks the present study's action in the abstract and at the pivot from gap to study. |
| Tense | Past for what was done and found (`we recorded`, `capacity increased`). Present for what evidence does now (`these results suggest`, `the data are consistent with`) and for established knowledge (`the hippocampus is thought to`). |
| Coined terms | Introduce in quotation marks once, define, then use bare. Never re-quote. |
| Register | Plain verbs, no ornament. Vivid words are allowed once per section at a structural moment (a paragraph-closing metaphor, an opening analogy) and never in a result sentence. |

## Openings

**Concrete first sentence.** Each Introduction opens on something a reader can picture: an aphorism, an analogy, the contrast between typical lab conditions and real experience, or a one-sentence definition of the technique. None opens on `X is important` or `In recent years`.

> Synthetic: *A lecture is heard once, yet it is remembered in pieces that recombine for years.*

**Historical anchor.** One clause naming the classic origin of the question, then a pivot to the present-day gap. Signals scholarship without a literature review.

**Analogy that sets up a hierarchy or contrast.** Used when the argument depends on levels or scales (words to chapters, seconds to minutes).

## Framing the gap

**Contrast pivot.** Paragraphs that summarize prior knowledge end with a turn: `However, ... remains unclear`, `Yet little is known about whether`, `is not agreed upon`. One turn per paragraph, at the end, so the reader carries the open question into the next paragraph.

**Trichotomy of causes.** The unresolved question is stated as a closed set of alternatives, often three: `reflect A, B, or a combination of the two`. This makes the study's job auditable: the Results must discriminate among the named options.

**Competing accounts, each with its own paragraph.** `One view holds that ... An alternative view is that ...`, each with citations, each ending on what it predicts. The present study is then positioned as the discriminating test.

**Conditional predictions before the design.** `If account A is right, we would expect X; conversely, if B, we would expect Y.` The prediction sentence precedes the method sentence, so the reader knows what to look for.

**Rhetorical question at a pivot.** One direct question, then the answer-in-brief: *Are events represented by content type? To answer this, we ...* Also used inside Results to motivate an analysis. At most one per section.

**Aim sentence with experiment map.** `The aim of the current study was to ...`, followed by one sentence per experiment stating what each adds (a larger independent cohort, a within-subject manipulation, a more realistic control). Closes with an explicit hypothesis pair: what should change and what should stay invariant.

## Results sentences

**Question-driven transitions.** Paragraphs open with the analysis question in first person: `We first tested the hypothesis that`, `We next asked whether`, `Finally, we asked whether`. Where the Introduction numbered hypotheses, the Results echo the numbers: *The third requirement of our theory is that ...*

**Reasoning stated before the result.** `We reasoned that if X, then Y should differ; if not, Y should be unchanged.` Announces the inferential logic so the number that follows is interpretable, and pre-commits the authors to a reading.

**Design-affordance sentence.** `This design allowed us to compare A and B within the same participants` or `This approach has the added benefit of ...`. Tells the reader why the method earns the inference.

**Result with `such that`.** Interactions and effects are reported with their direction attached in the same sentence: *a significant site-by-frequency interaction, such that capacity was higher when the frequency matched the site (F(1, 19) = 5.66, p = 0.028, η²p = 0.23).* No interaction is left directionless.

**`Driven by` for decomposition.** A significant omnibus effect is immediately explained: *This effect was driven by higher similarity for same-context pairs than for different-context pairs (pairwise, corrected p < 0.05).*

**Parallel frames for parallel results.** When the same analysis is repeated across regions, sites, or experiments, each paragraph uses the same syntactic template so differences are scannable. Repetition is deliberate; variety here is a defect.

**Null results in full, with the numerical direction.** *Reaction time did not differ between conditions (F(1, 19) = 1.32, p = 0.27), although it was numerically faster for matched stimulation.* The null gets its statistic and, where honest, its direction, then a consequence sentence: *Combined with the capacity effect, this argues against a speed-accuracy trade-off.*

**Pre-emptive control.** `To ensure that the difference was not due to window length, we repeated the analysis with a fixed window.` The alternative is named, the test is run, the result is reported in the same paragraph.

**Bridging explanation.** When a statistic is unfamiliar or a design is easy to misread, one plain restatement: `This interaction is formally identical to comparing matched versus mismatched trials`, `In other words, ...`, `That is, ...`.

**Compression markers.** `Briefly,` opens a one-paragraph methods recap at the start of Results; `see Methods` and `see Supplementary Fig.` close sentences that would otherwise expand.

**Exploratory flag.** `As an exploratory analysis, we ...` or `we did not have an a priori hypothesis about ...`, followed by an honest enumeration of the possible outcomes when there is no prediction.

**Tolerability first.** Stimulation and clinical studies open Results with one sentence on safety and tolerability.

**Summative closer.** Every Results paragraph ends with one sentence stating what the evidence licenses: `Together, these findings suggest`, `Taken together`, `Thus`, `This dissociation suggests`. The closer is hedged to the design (`suggest`, `is consistent with`, `indicate`) and never introduces a mechanism.

## Discussion sentences

**Findings summary with enumeration.** *There were four main findings. First, ... Second, ...* or a compact paragraph that restates each finding at Results strength. The verbs are no stronger than in Results.

**Replicate, extend, fail to replicate.** Explicit verbs that place the study in the literature: *replicates a growing number of studies*, *we extended these findings by showing*, *While we were not able to replicate X as reported by Y, we did observe Z*. A failure to replicate is paired with the design difference that might explain it.

**Alternative account, then bound it.** Raise the alternative in its own sentence (`This control analysis also raises a potential alternative account: ...`, `an alternative possibility is that`, `This could also be due simply to ...`), then either test it or state precisely what cannot be ruled out, then name the experiment that would settle it: *This possibility can be tested by ...*

**Scope disclaimers.** `We do not take this to suggest that ...`, `The present study was not designed to examine X; however, some insight may be gained ...`, `these findings speak to A but cannot speak to B`. The disclaimer draws the boundary of the claim in the author's own words before a reviewer does.

**Candid uncertainty register.** `we cannot rule out that`, `it is possible that`, `the origin of X is unclear`, `remains an open issue`, `should be interpreted with caution`. Each is followed by what would resolve it.

**Multi-line evidence chain.** When a conclusion rests on several observations: *Several lines of evidence support this. First, ... Second, ... Third, ... Finally, ...* One line per sentence, each a concrete observation.

**Limitation unit.** Four parts in one paragraph: the limitation, its consequence for a named claim, what was done to mitigate it, what future work should do. Signaled as `First, ... Second, ...` when several.

**Calibrated priority claim.** Once per paper, in the abstract or the first Discussion paragraph, and only when the design supports it: `provide the first direct evidence that`, `causally establish`. Elsewhere `provide further evidence`, `converging evidence`, `extend`.

**Big-picture closer.** The final Discussion paragraph zooms out to a principle the findings instantiate (`may represent a mechanism by which ...`, `a new framework for understanding ...`, `an efficient strategy for ...`), stated with a modal verb, followed by the last sentence of the paper naming what the work makes possible.

## Methods sentences

**Rationale voice.** Non-obvious choices get one sentence of reason: `We chose X to match Y`, `This procedure was designed to avoid floor and ceiling effects`. Only when the author supplied the reason.

**Candor about controls.** `Although X cannot serve as an ideal control, our earlier work showed it captures ...`. Naming a control's weakness in Methods pre-empts the reviewer and licenses the Discussion.

**Inline parameters.** Equipment, model, and settings sit in the sentence that names the step; no parameter is deferred to a table unless the venue requires one.

## Attention markers, rationed

`Notably`, `Critically`, `Importantly`, `Of note`, `Interestingly`: one to three per section across the exemplars. Reserve each for the result the argument turns on. `Critically` marks the test that discriminates between accounts; `Of note` marks an observation the reader might otherwise miss; `Interestingly` is the weakest and most often cut.

## Connectives that carry logic

- `Consistent with our predictions`, `In line with` : the result matched a stated prediction.
- `In contrast`, `Conversely` : a differing result, always followed by the difference in the same sentence.
- `Accordingly`, `Consequently`, `Therefore` : a consequence that actually follows.
- `Given that`, `Because` : a premise the reader already accepted.
- `Although ... , ...` : concede first, then assert; the assertion carries the sentence.
- `Despite` : a result that runs against an expectation named in the same sentence.

Use a connective only when the relation is real. A paragraph of consecutive `However`s is a sign that the argument has not been ordered.

## Rhythm

Long informational sentences carrying parameters or statistics alternate with short declaratives that state the point: *Capacity increased. Reaction time did not.* The short sentence usually follows the long one and states what the long one showed. Enumerations inside a sentence `(1) ..., (2) ..., and (3) ...` are used for hypotheses, findings, and reasons; bullets are never used in prose.

## What the exemplars do not do

- No `In this paper, we will` previews; the abstract and final Introduction paragraph do that work.
- No intensifiers without numbers; no `very`, `dramatic`, `massive`.
- No `novel` or `first` without a design that earns it.
- No paragraph that ends on a statistic; the closer is a sentence in words.
- No hedge stacking (`may possibly suggest`); one hedge per claim, matched to the design.
- No synonym rotation for technical terms; `pattern similarity` stays `pattern similarity`.
- No result introduced without its question; no question introduced without its answer in the same subsection.

## Applying the style under the evidence contract

Style moves shape sentences the author's materials already support. A summative closer cannot add a mechanism; an alternative-account sentence cannot invent the alternative; a limitation unit cannot invent the limitation; a priority claim needs the design. When a move calls for content the materials lack, leave the labeled gap and ask.
